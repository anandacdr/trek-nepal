Permits, fees and what a trek really costs

Which permits you need for each region, roughly what they cost, and an honest breakdown of where the money goes on a guided trek.

How the permit system works

Nepal's trekking permits come in three broad types, and which you need depends entirely on where you're walking.

  • Conservation area or national park entry fees — one per protected area you enter. Sagarmatha for the Everest region, ACAP for Annapurna, MCAP for Manaslu.
  • Local municipality fees — some regions now collect their own rural-municipality charge in addition to the park fee.
  • Restricted area permits — for controlled regions such as Upper Mustang, Manaslu and Dolpo. These are considerably more expensive, must be arranged by a registered agency, and legally require a licensed guide and a minimum of two trekkers.
Fees change — check before you budget

Permit prices and rules are set by government bodies and are revised periodically, sometimes at short notice. Treat every figure here as an order of magnitude, not a quotation. Whoever you book with should confirm current costs in writing.

Roughly what permits cost

Indicative figures for a foreign national, per person. SAARC nationals generally pay substantially less.

RegionPermits requiredIndicative cost
Everest / KhumbuSagarmatha National Park + local municipality fee~$50–60
AnnapurnaACAP entry permit~$25–30
ManasluRestricted area permit + MCAP + ACAP~$150+ depending on season
Upper MustangRestricted area permit + ACAP~$500 for 10 days

Restricted-area permits are usually priced per day, which is why Mustang and Manaslu carry so much more of the budget than the Everest or Annapurna regions.

Where the rest of the money goes

On a guided teahouse trek, permits are a modest share of the total. A rough shape of the costs behind a two-week Everest itinerary:

  • Internal flights — Kathmandu–Lukla return is one of the largest single line items and prices move with season.
  • Guide and porter wages, plus their insurance, equipment, food and lodging. Their costs continue on the days you're resting.
  • Teahouse lodging and meals — both climb steeply with altitude, because everything up there arrived on someone's back or a yak.
  • Permits and park fees, as above.
  • Transfers, Kathmandu hotels, and operator overhead — office, licensing, insurance, emergency response capability.

That last item is invisible until the day you need it, which is exactly why the cheapest quote is rarely the best one.

What you'll spend on the trail

Even on an all-inclusive trip there are personal costs. Budget roughly $10–20 a day, more if you like a hot shower.

ItemLower trailHigh altitude
Hot shower~$3–4~$5–8
Device charging (per hour)~$2–3~$4–6
Wi-fi / data card~$3–5~$5–10
Large bottle of water~$1–2~$3–4
Chocolate bar, soft drink~$2~$4–5

Everything gets more expensive the higher you go, for the obvious reason. Treating your own water rather than buying bottled saves a surprising amount and leaves less plastic behind.

Tipping, honestly

Tipping is customary on Nepali treks and forms a real part of guide and porter income, but it should never be a substitute for a fair wage — that's the operator's job, not yours.

Common practice is to tip as a group at the end of the trek, at roughly 10% of your trip cost split between the team, with the guide receiving somewhat more than each porter. Cash in rupees, handed over directly.

What matters more than the exact number: ask your operator whether their staff are insured and what they're paid. A company that answers that question immediately and specifically is one worth booking.

Insurance — the non-optional part

Standard travel insurance frequently excludes trekking above a stated altitude, often 3,000 or 4,000 m. That is below almost every route in this guide.

You need a policy that explicitly covers:

  • Trekking to your maximum planned altitude — check the number, not the marketing. Everest Base Camp requires cover to at least 5,600 m if you're going up Kala Patthar.
  • Helicopter evacuation and repatriation. A rescue flight from the Khumbu can run to thousands of dollars, payable upfront.
  • Medical treatment in Nepal and cancellation cover for the weather delays that Lukla makes routine.
Bring the paperwork

Carry your policy number and the insurer's 24-hour emergency line, both on your phone and on paper, and leave a copy with your guide. In an evacuation, someone will be asked for it before a helicopter lifts.
